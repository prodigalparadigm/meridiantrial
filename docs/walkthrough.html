What you asked for, and what we built

You asked

“We want AI to handle complaints.” 1,400 a month, three analysts, six days behind.

What we heard

The backlog is the visible problem. Regulatory complaints missing the 48-hour window is the expensive one. And nobody has agreed what “serious” means.

What we built

An intake assistant that reads, flags, routes and drafts. It does not send, and it does not decide what is serious. People still decide both.

The 48-hour window

Compliance needs to see a regulatory complaint within 48 hours of receipt. Nobody can tell a complaint is regulatory until someone reads it, and today the first read happens about six days in.

What each of you gets

Operations

A queue that is already read

  • Each complaint arrives with a summary, the issues raised, the owning department and a draft reply. Drafts passed every check in 98% of cases and are marked needs attention otherwise.
  • Routing to a defensible owner in 99% of complaints, with the reason shown and one-click reroute.
  • About $0.07 of model time per complaint, roughly $105 a month at current volume.
  • Time saved per complaint is not claimed here. The pilot measures it.
Compliance

Controls you can check yourself

  • Nothing can be sent. The software has no way to email or post anything. A test fails the build if one is ever added.
  • Two keys on flagged complaints. You acknowledge the flag, then a named analyst approves the reply. Only you can clear a flag.
  • Customer data stays in. Redaction runs on bank hardware, and a gate re-checks every outbound message. On the test month, 0 of 159 planted personal details reached the model. On a second month never used for tuning, 1 of 165 would have: a name written in capitals in a call note. That is why production adds a name detector on bank hardware, or runs inside Meridian's own cloud.
  • Every action on the record. A tamper-evident audit trail, and a view of exactly what was sent to the model for any complaint.

The model reads, extracts, routes and drafts. Ordinary code decides the order, the flags and the queue, so a model can raise a regulatory flag but can never lower one. If a step fails or the model declines, the complaint goes to a person. It never falls back to a different model.

The decision for Compliance: how flags are handled

Every regulatory complaint in the test month was flagged, and that is the floor. The price is false flags, and nearly all of them come from one place. When only the tripwire fired and the model read the complaint as not regulatory, the complaint was not regulatory in any of 25 cases. We propose two lanes. You decide how the second one is worked.

LaneWhat goes in itTest month
48-hour reviewThe model screener flagged it, or the screen could not run. Full review, as today.63 regulatory · 18 not
Tripwire checkOnly your tripwire terms fired; the model disagreed. Shown with the term and the model's summary; cleared or escalated in one click.0 regulatory · 25 not

Expected load at 1,400 complaints a month depends on how many are really regulatory, which the shadow pilot measures. If 15% are regulatory: about 20 a day in 48-hour review and 14 tripwire checks. If 25% are regulatory: about 26 a day in 48-hour review and 12 tripwire checks. The evidence file ranks every tripwire term by the false flags it caused, so you can narrow the noisiest ones, such as “fraud”, which also fires on fraud alerts that worked.

“Serious” is your call, and now it is written down

The model never ranks a complaint. It pulls out six facts and quotes the complaint for each: money at stake, a vulnerable customer, harm still happening, a deadline, an escalation threat, and repeat contact. A policy file turns facts into P1, P2 or P3. We wrote one policy for each senior analyst's view as we heard it. On the true facts they disagree on 65 of 163 complaints. That list is the agenda for one 30-minute meeting, and the agreed rule goes in the file.

The live demo

  1. Open the analyst queue. Ordered by the interim rule.
    Each row is already read: priority, a regulatory badge where it applies, owner and draft status. Switch the ordering between Analyst A's and Analyst B's rule to see the queue reshuffle.
  2. Open C-0010.
    A complaint mostly about something else, with a regulatory issue in one sentence. It was in the Compliance queue within seconds, with the quote that triggered it. The draft is acknowledgement only, and Approve stays locked until Compliance acknowledges.
  3. Open C-0008. Toggle “As received” and “What the model saw”, then open “What left the bank”.
    Replaced on this machine before anything left: 1 account, 1 address, 1 customer name, 1 phone. What you see under “What left the bank” is the exact text that was sent, from the log.
  4. Switch to Compliance. Acknowledge C-0010, then verify the audit chain.
    Your action is recorded with your name. Go back to the analyst view, edit the draft and approve it. The record keeps both the draft and the approved text.
  5. Open C-0006.
    The complaint tells “the AI” to treat it as routine. It was flagged anyway, the attempt is labeled for the analyst, and the draft ignores it.
  6. Open C-0004, then the Operations tab.
    One analyst's rule says P3 and the other's says P1. The calibration agenda lists every complaint like this one.
  7. Open C-0034, or type a new complaint with “New complaint”.
    Spanish complaints are flagged and routed like any other. The draft is in English so the approving analyst can read what they sign. A complaint typed live goes through every step in about a minute.
